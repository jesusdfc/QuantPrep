# Why Mean-Variance Optimization Often Produces Terrible Portfolios

## The setup

Markowitz mean-variance optimization (MVO) chooses weights $w$ to trade off return against
risk. Portfolio variance is:

$$
\sigma_p^2 = w^\top \Sigma w,
$$



and the unconstrained maximum-Sharpe (tangency) weights are proportional to
$\Sigma^{-1}\mu$. Given the *true* $\mu$ (expected returns) and $\Sigma$ (covariances),
this is genuinely optimal.

## The problem: garbage in, amplified garbage out

You never know $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ — you estimate them, with substantial error. MVO then:

- **Amplifies estimation error.** The $\Sigma^{-1}$ term loads heavily on the smallest
  eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, which are the **least reliably estimated**
  directions. Small input errors become large weight swings.
- **Is an error-maximizer.** The optimizer preferentially bets on assets that *look* high
  return / low risk — which is exactly what happens when noise inflates $\mu$ or deflates
  variance. It systematically over-weights the luckiest estimates.
- **Produces extreme, unstable weights.** Tiny changes in inputs (e.g. asset A's expected
  return $8\% \to 8.5\%$) can flip the portfolio dramatically. Estimation error dominates.

## What practitioners do

- **Shrinkage** of the covariance matrix (Ledoit-Wolf) toward a structured target.
- **Shrinkage / priors** on expected returns (e.g. Black-Litterman blends views with
  equilibrium).
- **Constraints:** long-only, position limits, turnover caps — these implicitly regularize.
- **Robust or resampled** optimization; or stepping back to **risk-based** allocations
  (equal weight, inverse-vol, risk parity) that ignore $\mu$ entirely.

## The interview tell

If asked "why not just run MVO?", the expected answer is about **estimation error and its
amplification**, the instability of $\Sigma^{-1}$, and the fact that simpler heuristics
often out-perform out-of-sample. That is a research-maturity signal.
