# Signal vs Noise — A Research Checklist

The recurring question in a quant research interview is some variant of:
*"You found a pattern. Is it real?"* A strong candidate answers with a **process**, not a
p-value.

## The pipeline you should think in

```text
Data → Universe → Feature construction → Labels → Train/test split →
Model → Backtest → Transaction costs → Risk → Robustness →
Out-of-sample → Live validation
```

Not:

```text
Data → model → Sharpe 2.4 🎉
```

## The checklist

1. **Hypothesis.** Is there an economic reason this should work? A mechanism beats a
   data-mined coincidence.
2. **Biases.** Survivorship (does the universe include dead names?), look-ahead (was every
   input available at decision time?), selection (how was the universe chosen?).
3. **Leakage.** Could any information from $t+1$ enter a feature at $t$?
4. **Multiple testing.** How many hypotheses did you try before this one? Deflate your
   significance accordingly.
5. **Out-of-sample.** Split by time; keep a final holdout you touch once.
6. **Economic significance.** A tiny but "significant" edge may vanish after costs. A
   p-value of 0.001 does **not** mean profitable.
7. **Costs & capacity.** Does the edge survive realistic transaction costs and position
   sizing?
8. **Robustness.** Does it hold across time periods, universes, parameters, markets, data
   vendors, and implementations? If it only works with one magic parameter, it is noise.
9. **Portfolio view.** What factor exposures does the signal accidentally load on? Is the
   "alpha" just repackaged market/size/value beta?
10. **Conclusion.** State plainly whether the evidence *is* or *is not* sufficient — and be
    comfortable concluding "not sufficient."

## The mindset

Deliberately try to **break your own result**: shuffle labels, insert placebo features,
change windows. A finding that survives sabotage is far more credible than one that merely
looks good once.
